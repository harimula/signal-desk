Defaults marked tested are the values the 33-year research used. experimental parts were never tested on history.

How settings workwhere they live and when they take effect

Settings are saved to the file bot/config.json in your GitHub repo. The bot re-reads that file once a minute while it runs, so most changes take effect within about a minute — no restart needed. Each setting has an allowed range (shown below); since 2026-10-24 the page refuses to save a value outside it and tells you which one, so what you see on the page is always what the bot runs. The sticky bar at the top shows unsaved changes until you press Save settings to the bot, then “Saved” with the time, or the exact error if GitHub refused.

Two kinds of values: a percentage of your money (so they scale if your stake changes) or fixed dollars (section 3 lets you choose per instrument). Where this page says “money” it means your stake if you set one in section 3, otherwise the whole practice account.

The running exampleused in every entry below

To keep the numbers real, every example on this page uses your current settings on the $100,000 practice account (no stake set) and a made-up stock, ACME, in a long uptrend at $100, well above its 200-day average of $90.

Your money is split stocks 40% ($40,000) · CALLs 10% ($10,000) · SPY quick options 10% ($10,000) · wheel 40% ($40,000, your change on the morning of 2026-10-30); PUTs and day trading are off. Preset Aggressive: risk per stock trade 1%, 12% of the money per stock, up to 8 stocks, 3 per sector. Buy level RSI-2 25 with intraday signals on; sell at +10%, or at the first close at least 0.5% above the buy price, or after 15 sessions; stop 25%; market filter on (no new dip buys while the S&P 500 is below its 200-day average). CALLs and PUTs sell only at +5%, at −90%, after 15 days or 3 days before expiry. SPY quick options trade 9:50 AM–2:00 PM on same-day expiries and sell at +5% / −35% or after 60 minutes.

The ACME trade in one line: Tuesday 11:20 AM, ACME has slid two days to $97 (RSI-2 = 18, below 25) → the bot buys 123 shares at $97.19 (≈$11,950) with a stop order at $72.89 → Wednesday it closes at $102.50, more than 0.5% above the $97.19 it paid → sold at the close: +$653 (+5.5%). If it had jumped to $106.91 (+10%) first, it would have sold right then. The entries below each take one setting and show what changes in this trade.

Pick Conservative while learning or with real money; Aggressive once the practice account has behaved for a few months.

Risk per stock trade

Default 0.5% tested · yours: 1% · range 0.05–3%

The most one trade may lose if it hits its normal stop, as a % of your money. The bot works backwards from this to decide how many shares to buy: shares = (money × risk%) ÷ (distance to the stop).

Example. 1% of $100,000 = $1,000 at risk. ACME at $97.19 with a 3× range stop $4.20 below (the RSI-2 rule’s own stop distance): $1,000 ÷ $4.20 = 238 shares allowed by risk. But section 3 caps money per stock at 12% = $12,000 → $12,000 ÷ $97.19 = 123 shares. The smaller of the two wins, so you buy 123. A jumpier stock with a $9 stop distance would get $1,000 ÷ $9 = 111 shares — fewer shares, same dollars at risk.

Lower it (0.25–0.5%) with real money. Raising it above 1% rarely adds anything, because the 12% money cap usually decides anyway.

Risk per option trade

Default 0.5% · yours: 1% · range 0.05–3%

Same idea for CALLs and PUTs: the most an option trade may lose at its stop (“Option stop”, section 4). Since 27 October 2026 the size follows the stop you set — before, it always assumed −50% — so a wider stop buys fewer contracts for the same risk.

Example. 1% = $1,000. A CALL at $3.50 ($350 a contract) loses $315 per contract at your −90% stop → $1,000 ÷ $315 = 3 contracts ($1,050). At a −50% stop it would lose $175 per contract → 5 contracts ($1,750). Section 3 also caps CALLs at 3% of the money ($3,000) per trade. For bigger CALLs with the −90% stop, raise this to about 1.8% (5 contracts again, $1,575 at risk).

Total risk cap

Default 3% · yours: 6% · range 0.5–15%

Adds up the risk-to-stop of everything open plus every waiting buy order. A new trade that would push the total over the cap is skipped (“total risk would exceed 6% of the account”).

Example. You hold 6 stocks each risking $1,000 = $6,000 = 6% of $100,000. The ACME buy would add $1,000 → 7%, over the cap → skipped, even though you have room for 8 stocks. Once one of the six sells, the next dip can be bought.

Max positions per sector

Default 2 · yours: 3 (the Aggressive preset; 5 before 2026-10-27) · range 1–10

How many stocks from one industry (Tech, Energy, Banks…) may be held at once. Dips often hit a whole sector on the same day; this stops you buying five oil stocks on one piece of oil news. The bot knows the sector of about 340 large companies (the ones the scan picks most); a stock it does not know counts as “Other” and is not limited.

Example. You hold NVDA, AMD and MSFT (3 Tech). AAPL dips → “skipped — already 3 positions in Tech”. An Energy dip the same day is fine.

Daily loss limit

Default 3% · yours: 5% · range 0.5–20%

With the money split on (section 3, the default) this is the stock part’s daily limit, measured on the stock money. When the stock part is down this much on the day, no new stock buys until tomorrow; stocks already held keep their normal exits. CALLs, PUTs and SPY have their own limits in section 3 / 4c and carry on.

Example. Stock money $70,000 × 5% = $3,500. A bad morning: three held stocks fall and the stock part is −$3,600 by 11:00 → “stocks down $3,600 today — past the 5% limit on its $70,000 — no new stock trades today; CALLs and PUTs and SPY options carry on”. One Telegram alert.

With the split off, this is one account-wide limit and it also closes all options.

Weekly loss limit

Default 6% · yours: 10% · range 1–30%

If the whole account is down this much since Monday’s open, no new trades of any kind until next week. Open positions are managed to their exits as usual. This one covers all parts together.

Example. Monday open $100,000; by Wednesday $89,800 (−10.2%) → “Down 10.2% this week (limit 10%) — no new trades until next week”.

Losing streak limit

Default 3 · yours: 4 · range 1–10

This many losing trades in a row in one part (stocks, CALLs or PUTs — counted separately) ends that part’s day for new trades. A bad tape tends to stay bad. A trade counts on the day it is sold, also when it was bought on an earlier day (since 27 October 2026; before, only trades bought and sold on the same day counted, so swing trades never did).

Example. Yours: 4. One morning a market drop takes four stocks bought earlier in the week through their −25% broker stop by 11 AM → 4 losing stock trades in a row → no new stock buys for the rest of the day; CALLs and SPY carry on, and the stocks still held keep their exits.

Example. Four stock trades close at a loss one after another by 1:30 PM → no more stock buys today; a CALL that wins in between does not reset the stock count, and the CALL part keeps trading.

3 · Capital allocationevery money decision in one place: your stake, and the shares for stocks, CALLs, PUTs, the SPY sleeve and the wheel

Money the bot works with (your stake)

Default empty = the whole account · yours: empty → $100,000 · range $0–$100,000,000

Enter the amount you actually intend to trade with. Every size, every loss limit and the pattern-day-trader rule are then computed on that figure instead of the practice account’s $100,000, and the Bot trades tab shows profit and loss against it.

Example. Stake $20,000 → the stock part is 40% = $8,000, a stock gets up to 12% of the stake = $2,400 (24 shares of ACME), the daily stock limit is 5% of the stock part = $400. Everything scales down together, so the practice run behaves exactly like the money you mean to use.

Set it before you judge the practice results — otherwise the bot is sized for $100,000 you may not have.

Trade stocks / Trade CALL options / Trade PUT options

Default on / on / off · yours: on / on / off (PUTs off since 4:58 PM on 2026-10-27) · PUTs experimental

Switch each instrument on or off. Stocks are the researched core. CALLs ride the same dips with options (limited loss, more leverage). PUTs are the mirror image — bought when a stock in a downtrend spikes up. Switched off, an option already held is still sold by its own rules.

What the October 2026 win-rate study found (they were off on your account for two hours that day; CALLs are back on at your choice, PUTs are off again since 4:58 PM): no CALL setting reached a high win rate at a profit — with model prices over 33 years CALLs on the same dips won about 52% of trades at +50% / −50% and at best about 7 in 10 with no stop and a long hold — about break-even to a small loss on average either way. The PUT side lost money in every test (stand-in: −8% a year in the out-of-sample years), and won 1 of its 6 real trades. On real option prices for 140 of the bot’s PUT signals since March 2024, the original exits won 34% and lost about 15% a trade; the CALL settings below raise that to about 66% won, still losing about 8% a trade — no setting reached 8 in 10.

Example. On the ACME dip the bot buys the shares (stocks on) and 3 CALL contracts (CALLs on). If a downtrend stock pops (RSI-2 above 75 with your level), PUTs on would buy a PUT on it. In the 5-year stand-in test, the PUT side lost money (−5% a year), which is why its share is small.

Where the money goes (the five shares)

Default stocks 55% · CALLs 30% · PUTs 10% · SPY 5% · wheel 0% (the wheel is off) · yours: stocks 40% · CALLs 10% · PUTs 0% · SPY 10% · wheel 40% (your saves on the morning of 2026-10-30: wheel 10 → 40%, CALLs 20 → 10%)

All five shares of your stake sit together here with a bar showing the split. You set the CALL, PUT, SPY and wheel shares; stocks always get the rest. The SPY share counts only while the SPY sleeve (4c) is on, the wheel share only while the wheel (4d) is on. Each part can never use more than its share, whatever the others do, and nothing is ever sold to rebalance — a full part simply skips new trades until money comes back. If the shares add up to more than 100% the page shows an error and Save is disabled until they fit.

Example. $100,000 → stocks $40,000, CALLs $10,000, PUTs $0 (switched off), SPY $10,000, wheel $40,000 (your split since the morning of 2026-10-30). Three stocks at ≈$12,000 each leave $4,000, so the fourth dip buy is cut to 41 shares of ACME and a fifth is skipped (“stock money $40,000, $40,000 already in use”) even though 8 positions are allowed. Meanwhile a CALL can still be bought: its $10,000 is separate, and the wheel’s $40,000 waits for its own sale window.

Prefer stocks: they have 33 years of evidence; options and SPY are the experiments. If a part keeps hitting “money … already in use”, give it more; if it keeps losing, give it less or switch it off.

Separate daily loss limits for stocks, CALLs and PUTs

Default on

On: each part has its own daily loss limit (stocks: section 2’s limit on the stock money; CALLs and PUTs: the options limit below on their own money; the wheel has none — its puts run for weeks) and its own losing-streak count, so a bad day in one never stops the others. Off: one shared pool and one account-wide daily limit; the shares still cap each part.

Example. CALLs lose $2,100 by noon (over 10% of $20,000) → CALLs stop and the open CALL is closed; the ACME stock buy at 1:00 PM still goes ahead. With the switch off, the same loss is only 2.1% of the account — under the 5% daily limit — so nothing stops at all.

CALLs share / PUTs share

Default 30 / 10% · yours: 10 / 0% (CALLs 20 → 10% on 2026-10-30, your save) · range 0–100% each

The part of your stake each option type may use together (see the four shares above).

Example. CALLs 20% = $20,000. Each CALL trade is also capped at 3% per trade ($3,000, below), so up to six or seven CALLs can be open before the share is used up.

SPY quick-options share

Default 5% · yours: 10% (since 2026-10-27) · range 0–50%

The money the SPY sleeve (section 4c) may use. Each SPY trade spends at most its “SPY money per trade” part of it (below; yours: 20%). Switch the sleeve off in 4c and this share goes back to stocks.

Example. Yours: 10% = $10,000, and 20% of it per trade = $2,000. A SPY CALL quoted $2.05 ($205 a contract) → $2,000 ÷ $205 = 9 contracts ($1,845). At your +5% take-profit (a sell order at $2.16) that is +$99; at the −35% stop ($1.33) −$648.

SPY money per trade

Default 100% · range 10–100% · yours: 20%

How much of the SPY share one SPY trade may use. Lower it to make each SPY trade — and each SPY loss — smaller, and to leave money for a second or third trade at the same time (see “SPY trades open at once” in 4c). At 100% one trade uses it all, so trades run one at a time whatever that setting says.

Example. SPY share 5% = $5,000; contracts at $2.05 ($205 each). 100%: 24 contracts ($4,920); a −15% stop costs about $744 (0.74% of the account). 50%: $2,500 → 12 contracts ($2,460); the same stop costs about $372. 20%: $1,000 → 4 contracts; about $124. The daily loss limit (25% of $5,000 = $1,250) and “2 losses in a row” still apply, so at 50% two losing trades cost about $744 instead of $1,488.

Wheel share

Default 10% · yours: 40% (your save, 7:10 AM on 2026-10-30; it started at 10%) · range 0–50%

The money the wheel may set aside. Every put it sells sets 100 × its strike aside, so it can always pay for the shares if they are delivered; shares it holds count at their market value. Like the SPY share it comes off the stock share, and only while the wheel is switched on — except that switched off it keeps the money it still has set aside until its open puts and shares are gone, so no other part spends it. In live mode it is scaled by the live size like every other part. If CALLs, PUTs, SPY and the wheel together pass 100%, the wheel gives way first when the bot reads the settings.

Example. Yours: 40% of $100,000 = $40,000, up to 3 companies, stocks $50–$300. The most-traded stock that fits gets the first put: a $190 stock’s put sets about $18,000 aside, a $150 one about $14,000 more, and the third put must fit in what is left (about $8,000 → a stock under ~$85). One expensive stock can still take most of the share — a $290 strike alone sets $29,000 aside, leaving room for only one small second put. If you want three spread evenly, set the Highest share price near $130 (three ≈ $12,500 puts fit in $40,000).

The wheel tied up cash for small, steady gains in the test; give it only money you are content to see in one or two stocks for a few months if a put is assigned.

Options daily loss limit

Default 10% · yours: 10% · range 1–50%

If CALLs lose this % of the CALL money in a day, new CALLs stop for the day; the same for PUTs on the PUT money. What happens to the CALLs already held depends on “When to sell a CALL or PUT” (section 4): with Also when the stock bounces back they are closed; with Only at its take-profit, stop or time limit (yours) they are kept to their own take-profit, stop, max hold and expiry — the test that chose those settings held every trade to its own exit, and selling them on a bad day would turn dips that usually recover into losses (since 27 October 2026).

Example. Yours: 10% of the CALL money ($20,000 = 20% of $100,000) = $2,000. CALLs are down $2,100 by 1 PM → no new CALL today; the five CALLs held keep their +5% take-profit orders at the broker and are not sold. Telegram: “… no new CALL trades today; open CALLs keep their own exits”.

Example. CALL money $20,000 × 10% = $2,000: once the CALL part is down $2,000 or more on the day, no new CALLs are bought until tomorrow; stocks and SPY options carry on. Your PUT share is 0% with PUTs switched off, so the PUT limit never comes into play.

Choose whether the money limits for that instrument are read as a percentage of the current money or as fixed dollar amounts. The pair you don’t pick is dimmed and ignored.

Example. Stocks in dollars, $5,000 per trade: ACME gets $5,000 ÷ $97.19 = 51 shares whatever the account size.

Default 10% / 2% / 1% tested (stocks) · yours: 12% / 3% / 2% · range 1–30% / 0.5–10% / 0.5–10%

The most one trade may spend. The actual size is the smaller of this and what the risk rule (section 2) allows.

All together — stocks / CALLs / PUTs (ceilings)

An optional hard ceiling on everything that instrument has open plus waiting orders. 0 means only the position counts and the shares above limit it. Since the shares already do this job, most people leave these at 0.

Example. CALL ceiling $4,000: with $3,500 of CALLs open only $500 is left, so the next ACME CALL is cut from 3 contracts to 1 ($350). With $3,700 open, $300 is less than one contract and the CALL is skipped (“call money ceiling $4,000, $3,700 already in use”).

Max stock positions / Max option positions

Default 6 / 4 tested (stocks) · yours: 8 / 6 · range 1–20 / 0–10

How many may be held at once. Option positions count the swing CALLs and PUTs together, held or being bought. SPY quick trades (they have “SPY trades open at once”), day trades and options you bought yourself do not count (since 27 October 2026; before, three open SPY trades took three of the places). When the limit is reached the log says so, and no contract is looked up until a place is free.

Example. Yours: 6. Five CALLs held and three SPY trades open → a sixth CALL can still be bought. Six CALLs held → the next dip gets no CALL (“the most at once”), while its stock can still be bought (8 stock places).

Example. You hold 8 stocks; ACME dips → “no slot”. Position count is checked before money, so even with money left the ninth stock waits.

4 · Strategythe tested values are the defaults — change with care

Which dips to buy first

Default Leaders first · or Deepest dip first

Decides the order when more stocks dip on the same day than the bot has room for. Leaders first buys the dips whose 3-month return beat the market (S&P 500) most; strong stocks that stumble bounce back fastest. In the corrected 33-year research it made a little more than the original order overall (+13.7% vs +12.4% a year at your sizes) but a little less in the last ten years (+4.2% vs +4.9%), with the same 65% win rate — no clear difference either way. Deepest dip first is the order used in the original test.

Example. A sell-off day: ACME (+8% vs the market over 3 months, RSI-2 18), BETA (−4% vs market, RSI-2 5) and GAMA (+2%, RSI-2 12) all dip, with room for two. Leaders first buys ACME and GAMA; deepest-dip would have bought BETA and GAMA.

When to sell a stock

Default On the bounce tested · or First profitable close tested 2026-10 · yours: First profitable close (since 2026-10-27)

The rule that sells a dip stock at the close (the take-profit and the stop work at any time on top of it; the max hold ends every trade). On the bounce — the original RSI-2 rule — sells once the stock closes above its 5-day average, even if that is a small loss: about 2 in 3 trades won in the 33-year test. First profitable close sells only once the stock closes at least “Profit needed to sell” above what the bot paid, and otherwise holds to the max hold.

The October 2026 win-rate study (33 years, 39 large companies; and the bot’s own scan list since 2016) found the profit rule wins about 82% of trades (15-session hold, market filter on), against 65% for the bounce, at about the same yearly return (+10.3% vs +10.6% a year over 33 years; +6.4% vs +4.3% since 2016; +1.8% vs +2.3% in the last 5 years) and with a smaller worst fall (−18% vs −25%). The catch: the trades that never recover are held to the end, so losses are rarer but larger — the average loss was about −5.9% instead of −2.7%; the average win about +1.7% either way. RSI-2 buy level

Default 10 tested · yours: 25 · range 2–30

RSI-2 is a 0–100 gauge of how hard a stock fell over the last 2 days: 100 × up-moves ÷ (up-moves + down-moves). Two down days = 0. The bot buys a stock above its 200-day average when RSI-2 drops below this level. Lower = rarer, sharper dips; higher = more trades on milder dips.

Example. ACME: Thursday $100.33, Friday $99.34 (−$0.99), Monday $100.00 (+$0.66) → up 0.66, down 0.99 → RSI-2 = 100 × 0.66 ÷ 1.65 = 40: no dip. No — at the tested level this dip would be skipped and the bot would wait for a deeper one. Two down days in a row give RSI-2 = 0: a buy at either level. Default off · yours: on (since 2026-10-30; share 40% since your 7:10 AM save) · tested 2026-10 on real option prices since February 2024

A separate part that sells options instead of buying them. Once a month it sells a put: it is paid a premium now, and in return it must buy 100 shares at the put’s price (the strike) if the stock ends under it — so it keeps 100 × the strike aside in cash (“cash-secured”). It spends only its own share (section 3) and never touches the other parts’ stocks or money. Switched off it sells nothing new; what is open keeps its buy-back order and runs to expiry, and shares it holds stay in the account.

What the October 2026 study found (bot/wheel_report.md, 32 monthly rounds from February 2024 to October 2026 on the options’ own daily prices): with these defaults 92% of puts made money on 39 large companies and the wheel earned about +7.9% a year on the money it had in use — while simply holding the same stocks made +15.7% and SPY +17.5%. Selling calls gives up the rise above their strike, and shares a put delivered still fall in a crash: the worst month was April 2025 (−6.8%), the worst single puts lost 20–35% of the cash set aside. With one to three stocks instead of 39, expect bigger swings.

Example (10% share = $10,000; ACME at $100). ACME stays up: two weeks later the put costs $0.75 → bought back, +$75; the money waits for the next window. In the next window it sells 1 call at the lowest strike at or above both $93.50 and 5% over $85 ($89.25) → say the $95 call for about $0.60 (+$60). How it decides

When: in the first three trading days after each monthly options expiration (the third Friday of the month, or the day before it when that Friday is a holiday), selling the option that expires at the next one, about five weeks out — and, with Sell between windows on (yours is), on any other day too, at the nearest monthly expiry at least 7 days out, so free money never waits weeks. The option: the highest put strike at least the strike distance under the price (the lowest call strike at or above both what the shares cost and the distance over the price), with a real quote (bid at least $0.05, the gap between bid and ask under 15% of their middle). It is offered at the middle of the quote; if nothing fills in 15 minutes it is priced again. New sales wait while the market check pauses the bot (a headline, the Fed hour, your Telegram /pause); buy-backs carry on. Strike distance

Default 5% · yours: 5% · range 0–20%

How far from the price the options are sold: a put this % under the price, a call this % over it. Farther away = a smaller premium but fewer losers.

Words used hereplain meanings

RSI-2 — a 0–100 gauge of the last two days’ move; low = a sharp drop. 200-day average — the average closing price of the last 200 trading days; above it = long-term uptrend. 5-day average — the bounce exit level. Stop — a price at which a position is sold to cap the loss. Take-profit — a price at which a winning position is sold. Risk (R) — the distance from entry to stop; “2 R” = twice that. VWAP — today’s average price weighted by volume; price above it means buyers are in charge. Spread — the gap between the price you can sell at (bid) and buy at (ask). Share (of the stake) — the slice of your money a part of the bot may use. Contract — one option, which covers 100 shares, so a $2.05 option costs $205. DTE — days to expiry; 0DTE = expires today. Pattern-day-trader rule — under $25,000, only 3 same-day round trips in 5 business days. Paper — the practice account. Premium — what an option sells for; the seller keeps it whatever happens. Strike — the price written into an option. Cash-secured put — a put sold with 100 × its strike kept aside to buy the shares. Covered call — a call sold on 100 shares already held. Assigned — the seller of an option that ends in the money must buy (put) or sell (call) the 100 shares at the strike. Monthly expiration — the third Friday of the month, when the standard monthly options expire.
